How to Check for a Hidden Leak With Your Water Meter

By the Temecula Pro Plumbing team · Published 2026-09-14

A water bill that's climbed without any obvious reason is the single most common way a hidden leak first shows up in a Temecula home. Before assuming the worst, or assuming nothing at all, there's a real test that takes about ten minutes and costs nothing: reading your own water meter. It won't tell you exactly where a leak is, but it will tell you, definitively, whether one exists and roughly which side of the meter it's on.

Finding the Meter

Every property served by Rancho California Water District has a meter, almost always located in a small box set into the ground near the street, close to the property line rather than the house itself. The box usually has a metal or plastic lid, sometimes marked "water meter," and it can be heavier than expected, so lifting it out rather than prying at an angle avoids cracking the lid. Inside, the meter face itself may be mechanical (a dial and a set of rolling numbers, like an odometer) or digital, depending on when it was installed.

The Basic Leak Indicator Test

Most meters, mechanical or digital, have a small leak indicator built into the display, often a triangular or star-shaped dial that's separate from the main numbers. Before testing anything, every fixture and water-using appliance in the house needs to actually be off: no dishwasher or washing machine mid-cycle, no irrigation system running, nobody filling a glass at the sink. Once everything is confirmed off, look at that small leak indicator. If it's spinning or moving at all, water is passing through the meter right now, even though nothing in the house should be using any, which means something is leaking somewhere between the meter and every fixture it feeds.

The Two-Reading Test, for a Slower Leak

A very slow leak, one small enough that the leak indicator's movement is hard to see with the naked eye, calls for a second method. Write down the exact numbers on the meter's main dial, then leave every fixture and appliance off for a set period, most guides recommend at least 30 minutes to a couple of hours for a genuinely slow leak, and avoid using any water at all during that window. Come back and read the numbers again. If they've changed even slightly, water moved through the meter during a period when none should have, which confirms a leak just as clearly as a moving indicator does, it just takes longer to catch a smaller one.

Narrowing Down Which Side of the House It's On

Once a leak is confirmed, the next useful step is figuring out whether it's on the service line between the meter and the house, or somewhere inside the house itself. Locate the home's main shutoff valve, usually near where the line enters the house, in a garage, utility closet, or exterior wall, and close it completely. With the main valve closed, go back to the meter and check the leak indicator again. If it's now still, the leak is somewhere inside the house, past that shutoff valve. If it's still moving even with the main valve fully closed, the leak is in the section of line between the meter and that valve, which is still the homeowner's responsibility to repair, but it changes where to actually start looking.

The Toilet Dye Test

A running or silently leaking toilet is one of the single most common causes of a leak that shows up on a meter test but isn't obvious anywhere else in the house, since a worn flapper can leak steadily into the bowl without ever making an audible running sound. A few drops of food coloring dropped into the tank, left for about ten minutes without flushing, will show up in the bowl water if the flapper isn't sealing properly. This costs nothing, takes ten minutes, and rules out or confirms one of the most likely causes before assuming something more serious, like a slab leak, is behind a rising bill.

What the Test Can't Tell You

The meter test is genuinely useful for confirming that a leak exists and roughly where it sits relative to the main shutoff, but it can't pinpoint an exact location inside a wall or under a slab. A leak that's confirmed to be inside the house, with the toilets ruled out by the dye test, and no visible dripping under any sink, is the point where electronic tracing equipment does the rest of the work, since water travels in ways that aren't intuitive once it's inside a wall cavity or under concrete. Homes in newer Temecula subdivisions like Winchester's 2000s-era construction and older homes near Old Town both benefit from the same meter test first, even though what the eventual leak turns out to be often differs by the home's age.

When to Stop Testing and Call Someone

A meter test that confirms a leak, with the toilets ruled out and nothing visible under any fixture, is the point to stop guessing. Continuing to run the two-reading test over and over doesn't get closer to the actual location, it just confirms what's already known. At that point, professional leak detection uses acoustic and tracer-gas equipment to find the specific point the meter test already proved exists, rather than repeating a test that's already given a clear answer.

FAQ

My meter's leak indicator isn't moving, but my bill still went up. Could I still have a leak?

Yes, particularly with a leak that starts and stops, like a toilet that only occasionally runs. The two-reading test over a longer window, or the toilet dye test specifically, catches leaks the instant snapshot of a leak indicator sometimes misses.

Is the leak before or after the meter my responsibility?

Everything from the meter to the house, including the meter box itself in most cases, is the homeowner's responsibility. Only the line before the meter, on the utility's side, belongs to the water district.
