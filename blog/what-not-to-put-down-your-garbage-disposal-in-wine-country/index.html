What Not to Put Down Your Garbage Disposal in Wine Country

By the Temecula Pro Plumbing team · Published 2026-09-14

Every garbage disposal guide covers the same short list: grease, coffee grounds, eggshells, fibrous vegetables. All of that advice is accurate and worth repeating, but Temecula Valley kitchens have two additional, genuinely local culprits that generic national lists never mention, because they're tied directly to what actually ends up in a wine country kitchen that wouldn't show up nearly as often somewhere else.

Grape Pomace: The One Nobody Warns You About

Home winemaking is a real, common hobby across the Temecula Valley, and crush season leaves behind pomace, the skins, seeds, and stems left over after pressing. Pomace looks like it should break down easily. It's soft fruit material, not a bone or a fibrous vegetable stalk. In practice, grape seeds are dense enough to resist a disposal's grinding ring the same way small pits do, and the skins behave like any other fibrous material once they're wet, wrapping around the impeller rather than breaking apart cleanly. A gallon of pomace disposed of a little at a time over several days, which is how a lot of home winemakers handle it, adds up to a real, recurring source of buildup that a onetime backyard barbecue's food scraps never would.

Avocado Pits and Skins, Especially Near Fallbrook

Backyard avocado trees are common enough across this region, and especially in the grove-heavy properties around Fallbrook, that homegrown avocados regularly outpace what a household can eat before more ripen. The pit is the obvious hazard, hard enough to damage a disposal's grinding components the same way a peach pit or mango pit would. The skin is the less-obvious one: it's tough and fibrous in a way that behaves more like a small fruit's rind than a soft vegetable peel, and it doesn't break down cleanly in a disposal any more than an orange peel does in quantity.

The Standard List Still Applies

None of this replaces the usual advice, it adds to it. Grease and cooking oil solidify in pipes as they cool, regardless of where the kitchen is. Coffee grounds compact into dense sludge rather than washing through cleanly. Eggshell membranes wrap around the impeller the same way pomace skins do. Starchy foods like pasta, rice, and potato peels swell after they're already down the drain, which is part of why a clog from starchy waste often shows up a day or two after the fact rather than immediately.

What to Do With Pomace and Avocado Waste Instead

Both are genuinely good compost material, better used than wasted, which makes the trash-or-compost answer better than the disposal-or-trash question most guides frame it as. Pomace composts well and is sometimes even sought after by home gardeners for its nutrient content once broken down properly. Avocado pits take longer to break down in a home compost pile than most kitchen scraps, but skins compost readily. Either way, keeping both out of the disposal avoids a buildup pattern that's easy to miss until it's already causing a slow drain. A five-gallon bucket kept near the kitchen sink specifically for pomace or avocado scraps during a heavy harvest or crush season makes the habit easier to keep than relying on remembering not to use the disposal for it in the moment.

Why This Buildup Is Easy to Miss Until It Isn't

The reason pomace and avocado waste cause problems disproportionate to how "soft" they look is timing. A single incident rarely causes a noticeable clog. The real issue is repetition over days or weeks during crush season or a heavy harvest, when the same kitchen sends material down the disposal in small amounts day after day. Each individual disposal cycle seems fine, and the slow buildup along the pipe walls doesn't announce itself until a drain that's been getting progressively slower finally backs up noticeably, at which point the accumulation has usually been building for longer than anyone realized.

When a Disposal Clog Has Already Happened

A disposal that's humming without spinning, or a kitchen sink draining slower than it used to following a crush season or a heavy avocado harvest, is worth addressing directly rather than running the disposal longer to try to force material through. Repeated attempts to grind material that's already wrapped around the impeller usually just adds motor strain without clearing anything. At that point, garbage disposal repair or, if the buildup has moved further down the line into the drain itself, drain cleaning addresses the actual blockage rather than continuing to run a jammed unit.

A Simple Rule of Thumb

If it's fibrous, seeded, or pit-bearing, it goes in the compost or the trash, not the disposal, regardless of how soft or "food-like" it looks. That single rule covers grape pomace and avocado waste just as well as it covers the standard list of eggshells, celery, and corn husks, and it's the rule worth remembering during crush season or a heavy avocado harvest specifically, when the volume of exactly this kind of waste spikes well above what a typical week's cooking produces. A little forethought about where that seasonal waste actually goes costs nothing and avoids the far more expensive alternative of a service call once the buildup has already turned into a real clog.

FAQ

Can I compost grape pomace at home?

Yes, and it's a genuinely good use for it. Pomace breaks down well in a home compost pile and adds real nutrient value, which is a better outcome than sending it through a disposal where the seeds and skins are more likely to cause buildup.

Is a little bit of avocado skin down the disposal really a problem?

A small amount occasionally is unlikely to cause an issue on its own, the same way a few onion skins wouldn't. The real risk is volume during a heavy harvest, when repeated small amounts accumulate into a genuine buildup pattern over days or weeks.
