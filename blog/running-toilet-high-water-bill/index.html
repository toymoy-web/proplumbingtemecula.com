Why Is My Water Bill So High? It Might Be a Running Toilet

By the Temecula Pro Plumbing team · Published 2026-09-14

A water bill that's climbed for no obvious reason sends most homeowners straight to worrying about a slab leak or a service line problem. Those are real possibilities, but the single most common cause, by a wide margin, is something far simpler and far cheaper to fix: a toilet that's running when it shouldn't be, sometimes so quietly that nobody in the house has ever actually heard it.

Why a Running Toilet Is So Easy to Miss

The version of a running toilet everyone recognizes is the loud, obvious kind, water audibly refilling the tank continuously. That version gets fixed quickly precisely because it's impossible to ignore. The version that quietly inflates a water bill for months is different: a worn flapper that isn't sealing completely lets water seep from the tank into the bowl in a thin, often silent stream, refilling the tank just often enough to avoid ever sounding like it's actively running. A household can live with this for a long time without ever consciously noticing it.

The Ten-Minute Test

Adding a small amount of food coloring to the tank water, then checking the bowl roughly ten minutes later without flushing in between, gives a direct answer. Color showing up in the bowl means the flapper isn't holding a seal and water is passing through continuously, even on a toilet nobody's ever heard actually running. The test costs nothing and takes only a few minutes, which makes it worth running on every toilet in the house rather than guessing at which one is the likely culprit, since a silent leak leaves no audible clue about its source.

How Much a Silent Leak Actually Costs

A running toilet doesn't have to be dramatic to add up. A continuously seeping flapper can waste meaningfully more water in a single day than an entire household's normal indoor use, simply because it never stops, twenty-four hours a day, every day, until it's fixed. Multiplied across a full billing cycle, that's often the entire explanation for a water bill that's climbed by what looked like an alarming amount with no other apparent cause.

What Actually Fails Inside the Tank

Two different parts commonly cause this, and they produce slightly different symptoms. A worn flapper, the rubber or silicone seal that lifts when the toilet flushes and should drop back into a watertight seal afterward, is the most common cause and is what the dye test specifically catches. A fill valve that isn't shutting off completely at the correct water level is the other common cause, and it tends to produce an actual audible running sound rather than a silent leak, since water is continuously overflowing into the tank's overflow tube rather than seeping past a bad seal. Both are inexpensive parts to replace, and neither requires replacing the whole toilet. A third, less common cause is a fill valve set too high to begin with, so that the tank refills past the overflow tube's opening even when the flapper and valve are both otherwise working correctly, and shows up as a running sound that starts right after every flush rather than intermittently throughout the day.

Why Flappers Wear Out in the First Place

A flapper's seal degrades from ordinary chemical exposure over time, not from any particular misuse. Chlorine and other water treatment chemicals slowly break down rubber and silicone seals, and the flapper's constant contact with tank water means it's exposed to that degradation continuously rather than intermittently the way a supply-line gasket might be. A flapper that's five or more years old is a reasonable age to suspect even without other symptoms, simply because that's roughly when this kind of wear starts becoming noticeable in a lot of standard toilets, though the actual lifespan varies by manufacturer and water chemistry.

Why This Matters More in a Multi-Toilet House

A larger newer-construction home with several bathrooms, the kind common in newer Menifee subdivisions, has more chances for one toilet's flapper to fail quietly while the household keeps using the other bathrooms normally, which is part of why the dye test is worth running on every toilet rather than just the primary one. A single silently leaking flapper in a guest bathroom that rarely gets used can run for months completely unnoticed, since there's no daily usage pattern to make the water level or sound seem unusual.

When It's Not the Toilet

If every toilet in the house passes the dye test cleanly and the bill is still climbing, the next step is the water meter test: shut off every fixture and appliance and watch the meter's leak indicator directly. A leak that shows up there once every toilet is ruled out points toward a fixture leak elsewhere, a slab leak, or a service line issue, each of which needs a different kind of diagnosis. But given how often the answer turns out to be a ten-dollar flapper, it's worth ruling out first, before assuming the more expensive explanation.

Fixing It Once It's Confirmed

A confirmed flapper or fill valve issue is a straightforward toilet repair, matched to the specific worn part rather than a blanket toilet replacement. For a toilet old enough that this isn't its first internal repair, replacing the whole unit sometimes makes more sense than repeating individual part replacements, particularly on an older, higher-flow model where the water savings from a newer toilet add a second real benefit beyond just stopping the leak.

FAQ

How do I know which toilet is leaking if I have more than one?

Run the dye test on every toilet in the house, not just the one that seems most likely, since a silent flapper leak gives no obvious clue about which fixture it's in. Testing each one individually is the only reliable way to isolate it.

Can a running toilet really explain a large jump in my water bill?

Yes. A continuously seeping flapper runs nonstop, all day and all night, which adds up to a significant volume of wasted water over a full billing cycle, often enough on its own to explain a bill increase that initially looks alarming.
