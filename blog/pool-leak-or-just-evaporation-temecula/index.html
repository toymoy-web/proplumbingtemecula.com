Pool Leak or Just Evaporation? The Bucket Test for Temecula's Climate

By the Temecula Pro Plumbing team · Published 2026-09-14

A pool that needs topping off more than it used to raises an obvious question: is that a leak, or just normal evaporation? The generic answer floating around most pool guides, a quarter to half inch of water loss per day is normal, more than that means a leak, is a reasonable starting point almost anywhere. It's a less precise answer here, because Temecula's afternoon wind pattern genuinely pushes evaporation higher than that generic baseline on a lot of summer days.

Why Temecula's Evaporation Rate Runs Higher Than the Generic Number

The same Rainbow Gap airflow that keeps Temecula's wine country climate comfortable, cool ocean air pulled in through the gap in the coastal mountains most afternoons, moves real, sustained wind across an uncovered pool surface on exactly the days summer heat would otherwise push evaporation up on its own. Wind is one of the primary drivers of evaporation rate, alongside heat and low humidity, and Temecula's summer afternoons regularly combine all three at once: highs near 90 degrees, low humidity, and a consistent afternoon breeze. A pool here can reasonably lose closer to three-quarters of an inch to a full inch on a hot, breezy summer afternoon and still be within normal evaporation, well above the quarter-inch figure that generic guides quote for a calmer, more humid climate.

Running the Bucket Test Correctly Here

The test itself doesn't change: fill a five-gallon bucket with pool water to about an inch from the top, set it on a pool step so it's partially submerged, and mark the water level both inside the bucket and on the pool's tile or skimmer face. After 24 hours, compare how much each dropped. If the pool and the bucket dropped roughly the same amount, that's evaporation, since both are exposed to the same sun and wind. If the pool dropped noticeably more than the bucket, water is leaving through something evaporation doesn't explain.

Running It Twice: Pump On, Then Pump Off

A single run of the test only tells you whether a leak exists, not where. Running it once with the pump on its normal schedule and again with the pump off for a full 24 hours narrows that down. A leak that shows up clearly with the pump running but disappears or shrinks with the pump off points toward the pressurized plumbing or equipment side, exactly what pool equipment leak detection is built to isolate. A leak that persists at the same rate regardless of whether the pump is running points more toward the shell or a waterline fitting rather than the plumbing.

Why This Matters More in Pool-Heavy Neighborhoods

Communities with a high concentration of pools built around the same time as the surrounding homes, Menifee's newer subdivisions among others regionally, see equipment seals and unions reach the same age across many pools within a similar window. A homeowner in one of these neighborhoods who's heard a neighbor recently deal with a pool leak has a reasonable, evidence-based reason to run the bucket test proactively rather than waiting for a water bill to prompt it, since the same age-related wear pattern that affected one pool nearby is a real, plausible factor for others built in the same period.

Other Signs Worth Checking Alongside the Test

A few physical clues are worth checking too, since some of them show up before a bucket test would catch a genuinely small leak. Soggy grass or a soft, spongy patch of ground anywhere near the equipment pad is one of the more reliable early indicators, since that's usually water finding its way to the surface from a fitting or union somewhere underneath. A pump that's kicking on and off more often than its normal schedule, chemical costs creeping up to hold the same water balance, air bubbles visible at a return jet, or a pressure gauge reading noticeably lower than usual at the same setting all point toward the equipment or plumbing side specifically, worth mentioning to whoever runs the actual diagnosis so they know where to start looking first.

Refilling Less Often Isn't a Fix

Simply topping off a pool that's losing water faster than normal evaporation delays the actual problem rather than solving it. Beyond the immediate cost of the added water itself, a leak on the equipment or plumbing side often gets worse gradually rather than staying constant, since whatever caused the initial failure, a worn union, a degraded seal, continues to degrade under the same conditions that caused it in the first place. A leak that's manageable with occasional refilling this season can become a larger, more disruptive repair a year or two later if the underlying cause is never actually addressed.

What to Do Once a Leak Is Confirmed

A bucket test that comes back showing real loss beyond normal evaporation is the point to move from DIY testing to pressure testing and dye tracing, since the bucket test confirms a leak exists but can't pinpoint where. Continuing to top off the pool and re-running the bucket test repeatedly doesn't get closer to an answer once a leak's already confirmed, it just delays the point where the actual leak gets located and fixed.

FAQ

How much water loss is actually normal for a Temecula pool in summer?

Meaningfully more than the generic quarter-inch-a-day figure quoted for milder climates. On a hot, breezy summer afternoon, three-quarters of an inch to a full inch of evaporation can still be entirely normal here, which is why the bucket test, comparing the pool against a bucket exposed to the same conditions, is more reliable than any flat daily number.

Does the bucket test work in winter too?

Yes, though evaporation rates run lower in cooler, calmer weather, so a smaller gap between the pool and the bucket is more meaningful in winter than the same gap would be during a hot, windy summer stretch.
