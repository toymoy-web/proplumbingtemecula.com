Faucet & Fixture Repair in Temecula, CA

A dripping faucet or a shutoff valve that won't fully close usually means a worn cartridge or valve seat. We repair or replace it in Temecula, CA. Call (951) 467-1450.

Faucet and fixture repair covers kitchen and bathroom faucets, shutoff valves, and the shower or tub valve behind the wall, whenever the issue is a drip, a handle that's hard to turn, or a valve that won't fully shut off. Most of these repairs come down to a single worn internal part rather than the whole fixture failing.

What causes this in Temecula

Temecula's water, delivered through Rancho California Water District, runs moderately hard, and that mineral content is the single biggest reason a faucet cartridge or valve seat wears out faster here than it would in a soft-water region. A cartridge-style faucet, standard in most homes built since the 1990s, develops mineral buildup around the cartridge's seals that eventually causes dripping or a stiff handle. Older compression-style faucets, more common in original fixtures in Old Town-adjacent and Vail Ranch homes, wear out at the rubber washer instead, which is a simpler and generally cheaper part to replace.

Signs to watch for

A faucet that drips steadily even with the handle fully off almost always means a worn cartridge, washer, or valve seat rather than anything wrong with the supply line itself. A handle that's become stiff or requires more force to turn is an early sign of the same mineral buildup, often showing up before the drip does. A shutoff valve under a sink that won't fully stop water flow when closed, or one that's seized and won't turn at all, is worth addressing before it's actually needed in an emergency, not after.

How it's diagnosed

Most faucet and valve issues are confirmed by removing the handle and inspecting the cartridge, washer, or valve seat directly rather than guessing from the symptom alone, since a drip and a stiff handle can both stem from the same worn part or from two different ones on the same fixture. A shutoff valve that won't close gets tested under actual water pressure to confirm whether it's seized from corrosion or the seal itself has failed.

Repair options

A worn cartridge or washer is a straightforward like-for-like replacement in nearly every case, and it's the far more common outcome than replacing the whole faucet. Replacement of the entire fixture makes more sense when the faucet body itself is corroded, when replacement parts for an older or discontinued model are difficult to source, or when the homeowner is already planning a fixture upgrade and the repair cost approaches what a new fixture and installation would run.

Tightening the handle harder vs. Cartridge or washer replacement

Tightening the handle harder

  • Doesn't address a worn cartridge or washer
  • Can strip the handle or valve stem over time
  • A drip usually gets worse, not better, without a part replacement
  • Wastes real water while the underlying part keeps degrading

Cartridge or washer replacement

  • Matched to the fixture's actual brand and model
  • Fixes the drip at its actual source
  • Shutoff valves checked at the same time, since they wear similarly
  • Whole-fixture replacement only recommended when it's genuinely warranted

What affects the price

Fixture typeA cartridge faucet and a compression faucet use entirely different repair parts and take different amounts of time.
Part availabilityA common, widely stocked brand's cartridge is quicker to source than a discontinued or specialty model's.
Repair vs. replaceA fixture already showing surface corrosion or plating wear alongside the internal issue is often better replaced than repeatedly repaired.

FAQ

Why does my faucet still drip after I tightened the handle?

A drip almost always comes from a worn cartridge, washer, or valve seat inside the faucet, not from the handle itself, so tightening the handle harder doesn't fix it and can eventually strip the valve stem.

Should I repair or just replace an older faucet?

A cartridge or washer replacement is usually the better value if the faucet body itself is in good shape. Replacement makes more sense once the finish is corroded, parts are hard to find for an older model, or the repair cost gets close to what a new fixture would cost installed.

My under-sink shutoff valve won't turn. Is that urgent?

A seized shutoff valve isn't an emergency by itself, but it means there's no way to isolate that fixture quickly if it ever does leak or fail, so it's worth having it freed up or replaced before it's actually needed rather than discovering it's stuck during a real leak.
